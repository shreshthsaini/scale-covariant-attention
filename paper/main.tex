\begin{abstract}
Diffusion transformers trained near one megapixel fail at higher resolutions: attention meets unseen token counts and position offsets, and its cost grows quadratically. Each head's balance between local detail and global layout, we find, is measurable at the training resolution and transfers to larger images. We introduce Scale-Covariant Attention (\sca{}), which calibrates once, fitting one weight per head and noise level in closed form against the model's full attention, and reuses that table at the target. Each head mixes a native-size sliding window with block-centre global attention, both in the trained position range, after a short global-only opening that sets the layout. For FLUX.1-dev at $4096^2$, \sca{} is the strongest single-pass training-free operator we tested. It beats every shared-sampler baseline on all per-image metrics, has the best FID and fine detail of eight methods, and runs $2.85\times$ faster than AReA. Dropped into HRDiT's progressive schedule, it matches HRDiT's preference scores in less than half the time. Gains hold at $6144^2$, with two more seeds, and on FLUX.2-dev; on FLUX.1-Krea-dev \sca{} matches AReA. Code: \codeurl.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Diffusion transformers~\citep{peebles2022dit} such as FLUX.1~\citep{flux2024} and Stable Diffusion~3~\citep{esser2024sd3} are trained at about one megapixel. At $4096\times4096$ the image-token count grows 16-fold and full-attention cost about 256-fold. Every attention layer then sees token counts and position offsets outside its training distribution. Outputs show duplicated subjects, broken layouts, and blurred texture~\citep{he2023scalecrafter,du2023demofusion,issachar2025dype,rajabi2026area}. In FLUX.1-dev at $4096^2$, full attention collapses into stripes or a flat field (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}).

Training-free fixes edit the positional encoding~\citep{zhuo2024lumina,issachar2025dype,zhao2025ultraimage,rajabi2026sega}. Others restrict the keys each query sees~\citep{zhang2023hidiffusion,ma2025resdit,xue2026hrdit,rajabi2026area}. Restricting keys keeps position offsets in range, but a native-size window cannot see an object larger than itself. At high noise, when the layout forms, windows settle it independently and repeat the subject (Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}a). AReA~\citep{rajabi2026area}, the most recent key-restricting method, therefore attends to keys subsampled onto a native-size grid for the first 8 of 28 steps. It then attends to windows that shrink to native size, and all heads share this schedule. Head-specific receptive fields are also established: HRDiT assigns per-head scopes under a cost budget~\citep{xue2026hrdit}, and UltraImage concentrates attention per head using entropy~\citep{zhao2025ultraimage}. We ask whether a head's local/global output mixture can be fitted at native resolution and transferred to larger images.

We answer it by emulation. To mimic a target $s$ times larger, we shrink both a native-size neighbourhood and a downsampled view of the whole image by $s$. The image-to-window ratio then matches the target, while full attention stays in distribution and serves as ground truth. A convex mix of the two views fits each head well. The fitted weight differs strongly between heads and is stable across tested prompt splits (correlation 0.99). It also transfers: the table fitted at emulated scale 2 explains scale 4 with 2.4\% more output error than the scale-4 table, and a $1024^2$ table explains $1280^2$ with 0.8\% more (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}). We call this measured transfer scale covariance. The local view follows a law fixed in tokens and the global view a law fixed in image fractions, and over the tested scales the weight between them stays nearly constant.

We introduce Scale-Covariant Attention (\sca{}), which turns this measurement into an attention operator (Figure~\ref{fig:method}). Each head uses its calibrated weight to mix two branches. The global branch attends to block-centre keys on the native grid with rescaled coordinates; the local branch is an exact native-size sliding window. The calibrated mix alone does not preserve layout: applied from the first step at $4096^2$, it draws five Moai statues for a prompt asking for one (Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}a). \sca{} therefore runs the global branch alone for the first 8 of 28 steps (the opening phase), as a frequency argument suggests (Argument~\ref{arg:freq}).

To summarize, we measure each head's local/global mix at the training resolution and reuse it at the target. Against seven baselines with paired bootstrap intervals, \sca{} beats AReA on every per-image metric at $4096^2$, at 71.1\,s per image against 202.4\,s (Section~\ref{sec:main}). Inside HRDiT's progressive schedule it matches HRDiT's preference scores in less than half the time. We highlight our main contributions below.
\begin{itemize}
  \item We fit each head's mix of local and global attention in closed form at the training resolution and show that the fit transfers across scales (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).
  \item We propose \sca{}, a per-head mix of a native-size window and block-centre global keys whose offsets stay in the trained range at a cost linear in the token count.
  \item We show that at $4096^2$ on FLUX.1-dev, \sca{} beats AReA on 11 of 15 metrics with paired 95\% intervals excluding zero, loses none, and runs $2.85\times$ faster.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scale-Covariant Attention}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Setting and notation}
\label{sec:setting}
A multimodal diffusion transformer such as FLUX.1 concatenates $T$ text tokens $\Tset$ with $N=HW$ image tokens on the grid $\Gset=[H]\times[W]$, where $[n]=\{0,\dots,n-1\}$. Each block applies joint attention (57 blocks of 24 heads in FLUX.1). It is trained at about $1024^2$ pixels, a native grid of $n_0\times n_0$ tokens with $n_0=64$. Rotary embeddings~\citep{su2021roformer} give token $j$ a position $p_j\in\mathbb{Z}^2$ (FLUX.1 puts all text tokens at $0$) and a rotation $R_p$ with $R_p^\top R_{p'}=R_{p'-p}$. For one head, a pre-rotation query $q$ placed at $u$, and a set $\Kset$ of (key, value, key position) triples,
\begin{equation}
  \Attn(q,u;\Kset) \;=\; \textstyle\sum_{(k,v,u')\in\Kset} \operatorname{softmax}_{\Kset}\!\big(q^\top R_{u'-u}\,k/\sqrt d\big)\; v ,
  \label{eq:attn}
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the head dimension. Full attention is $\Ofull_i=\Attn\big(q_i,p_i;\{(k_j,v_j,p_j)\}_{j\in\Tset\cup\Gset}\big)$. The target grid is $H=s_H n_0$, $W=s_W n_0$ with integer factors ($s$ when equal), so full attention meets offsets $s$ times the trained range. The latent at noise level $\sigma$ is $x_\sigma=(1-\sigma)x_0+\sigma\epsilon$. Splitting the keys into a near and a far set gives exactly $\Ofull_i=m_iO^{\mathrm{near}}_i+(1-m_i)O^{\mathrm{far}}_i$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:split}). Here $O^{\mathrm{near}}_i$ and $O^{\mathrm{far}}_i$ attend only to their own set, and $m_i$ is the softmax mass on the near set. Every head is thus a query-dependent mix of a near and a far field. \sca{} approximates this mix with two separately normalized operators and one weight per head. Because both branches include text keys and their image key sets can overlap, the identity does not decompose \sca{} exactly.

\subsection{Two operators with native-range image geometry}
\label{sec:branches}

\paragraph{Global operator.} Let $\Gnat=[H/s_H]\times[W/s_W]$ be the $n_0\times n_0$ grid of blocks. Block-centre subsampling keeps for each block $b$ the token at $\kappa(b)=(s_H b_1+\lfloor s_H/2\rfloor,\, s_W b_2+\lfloor s_W/2\rfloor)$. The coarse map $c(p)=(\lfloor p_1/s_H\rfloor,\lfloor p_2/s_W\rfloor)$ sends a position to its block, so $c(\kappa(b))=b$, and
\begin{equation}
  \Kset^{\mathrm{glob}}=\{(k_j,v_j,0)\}_{j\in\Tset}\cup\{(k_{\kappa(b)},v_{\kappa(b)},b)\}_{b\in\Gnat},\qquad
  \Oglob_i=\Attn\big(q_i,\,c(p_i);\,\Kset^{\mathrm{glob}}\big),
  \label{eq:glob}
\end{equation}
with $c(p_i)$ replaced by 0 for text queries. This is AReA's first phase with the pooling factor fixed at $s$. It models content whose extent is a fixed fraction of the image: layout, object count, identity.

\paragraph{Local operator.} Image queries form tiles of $t\times t$ tokens ($t=8$); query $i$ lies in tile $\tau=\lfloor p_i/t\rfloor$. The window side is $w=\min(n_0,H)$. The window of tile $\tau$ starts at $a(\tau)=\clip\big(t\tau+\lfloor t/2\rfloor-\lfloor w/2\rfloor,0,H-w\big)$ per axis, centred on the tile and clamped to the grid. With $\Win(i)$ the $w\times w$ window of $i$'s tile,
\begin{equation}
  \Kset^{\mathrm{loc}}_i=\{(k_j,v_j,0)\}_{j\in\Tset}\cup\{(k_j,v_j,p_j)\}_{p_j\in\Win(i)},\qquad
  \Oloc_i=\Attn\big(q_i,\,p_i;\,\Kset^{\mathrm{loc}}_i\big),
  \label{eq:loc}
\end{equation}
an exact sliding window with the original positions. It models content whose extent is fixed in tokens: edges, texture, fine structure.

\begin{proposition}[Trained position range]
\label{prop:range}
Assume exact integer position coordinates and $t\le n_0\le\min(H,W)$. Every query-key offset of the global operator and every image query-key offset of the local operator is at most $n_0-1$ per axis. Each image query sees $T+n_0^2$ keys in either operator, as in full attention on the native grid.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Cost]
\label{prop:cost}
Per head and layer, full attention scores $(N+T)^2$ query-key pairs, the global operator $(N+T)(T+N_0)$ with $N_0=|\Gnat|=n_0^2$, and the local operator $N(T+w^2)$. For fixed $n_0$ and $T$ the total is $O\big(N(T+w^2+N_0)\big)$, linear in $N$.
\end{proposition}
Proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. Proposition~\ref{prop:range} assumes exact coordinates; the frozen FLUX.1 implementation rounds positions in bf16 beyond 256 tokens per axis (Appendix~\ref{app:impl}). Native-range geometry also does not make hidden states in-distribution. Text-image offsets in the local operator equal $p_i$, as in full attention at the target (Remark~\ref{rem:text}). At $4096^2$ \sca{} scores 7.2 times fewer query-key pairs than full attention, 8.4 times fewer with the opening phase (Table~\ref{tab:flops}).

\subsection{Per-head mixing and the opening phase}
\label{sec:mix}
For an image query of head $h$ in block $l$ at noise level $\sigma$,
\begin{equation}
  O_i \;=\; \Oglob_i \;\;\text{if } \sigma>\sigma_g,\qquad
  O_i \;=\; \pi_{lh}(\sigma)\,\Oloc_i + \big(1-\pi_{lh}(\sigma)\big)\,\Oglob_i \;\;\text{otherwise},
  \label{eq:mix}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_g$ is the gate and text queries use $\Oglob_i$. The weight $\pi_{lh}(\sigma)$, written $\pih$, stands in for the near-field mass $m_i$; one weight per head is cheap and a few prompts determine it.

\begin{argument}[A frequency-based explanation for the opening phase]
\label{arg:freq}
At noise level $\sigma$ a spatial frequency band has signal-to-noise ratio $\big((1-\sigma)/\sigma\big)^2 S(f)$ for image spectrum $S$. If latent power falls with frequency, only low frequencies carry signal at high noise. A window of $w$ tokens spans less than one period of any frequency below $1/w$. Independently attended windows may then repeat local structures instead of forming one large object, while a global branch supplies context across windows. Hidden states also carry information from earlier layers, so this is a mechanistic hypothesis, not an impossibility result (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}).
\end{argument}
Output matching cannot see this failure: a tokenwise squared error can favour local detail without preserving layout. We have not measured per-band signal-to-noise ratios of FLUX.1 latents, but a 16-token window gives smaller and more numerous copies than a 64-token one, as predicted (Figure~\ref{fig:failure}). We set $\sigma_g=0.89$ (the first 8 of 28 steps at maximum shift, as in AReA). Gating 13 steps gives a $4\times4$ block mosaic, because all queries of a block share one coarse position.

\subsection{Calibration by output matching}
\label{sec:calib}
The weights must come from where the model is trustworthy, which the target is not. On an in-distribution $n\times n$ grid ($n=64$ at $1024^2$), emulated scale $s_c$ uses subsampling factor $s_c$, window side $n/s_c$, and tile side $\mathrm{round}(tn/(s_cn_0))$. The ratio of grid to window is then $s_c$, as at a target of scale $s_c$. The sampler runs unmodified full attention; at every block and step both emulated operators are evaluated on the same queries, keys, and values. For block $l$, head $h$, and noise bin $\beta$, sums over prompts, steps with $\sigma\in\beta$, image queries, and channels give
\begin{equation}
  A=\textstyle\sum\|\Oloc-\Oglob\|^2,\quad
  B=\sum\langle \Ofull-\Oglob,\,\Oloc-\Oglob\rangle,\quad
  E=\sum\|\Ofull-\Oglob\|^2,
  \label{eq:stats}
\end{equation}
where $\Oloc$ and $\Oglob$ use the emulated geometry. The squared error of the mix with weight $\pi$ is $J(\pi)=E-2\pi B+\pi^2A$.

\begin{proposition}[Closed-form calibration and transfer cost]
\label{prop:opt}
In each cell $(l,h,\beta)$, $J$ is minimized on $[0,1]$ by $\pi^\star=\clip(B/A,0,1)$, with $\pi^\star=1$ if $A=0$. Any other weight $\pi'\in[0,1]$ (fitted at another scale or resolution, per layer, shared, or a hard switch) has $J(\pi')-J(\pi^\star)\ge A(\pi'-\pi^\star)^2$, with equality when $0<\pi^\star<1$.
\end{proposition}

# Output: pi*[s_c, l, h, beta]; code in App. (*@\ref{app:code}@*)
def calibrate(q, k, v, l, sigma):  # every block
  O_full = attn(q, k, v)        # Eq. (*@\ref{eq:attn}@*), all keys
  beta = min(int(14 * sigma), 13)    # noise bin
  for s_c in (2, 4):            # emulation, App. (*@\ref{app:calib}@*)

    t_c = round(t * w / n0)     # tile t n/(s_c n0)
    O_glob = global_branch(q, k, v, f)    # Eq. (*@\ref{eq:glob}@*)

    E[s_c, l, :, beta] += sum(df * df)

pi = fill_empty_bins(pi)      # nearest bin

So the per-head table has the lowest output error of any rule constant within a cell, and replacing it costs an $A$-weighted squared distance. This holds at the emulation scale; Section~\ref{sec:analysis} measures how well tables transfer between scales and resolutions. The same sums score every alternative rule without another forward pass. We use 14 noise bins of width $1/14$ and interpolate linearly between the mean noise levels of occupied bins at inference. One pass collects emulated scales 2 and 4. A target with factors $(s_H,s_W)$ uses the table whose scale is nearest $\max(s_H,s_W)$ in log space, here always 4. The local operator is one FlexAttention call~\citep{dong2024flex} over tile-major tokens with the window as an exact block mask (Algorithm~\ref{alg:sample}, Appendix~\ref{app:impl}).

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\sca{}}{SCA} inside a progressive schedule}
\label{sec:progressive}
\sca{} replaces only the attention, so it can serve inside a multi-stage sampler. HRDiT~\citep{xue2026hrdit} denoises at $1024^2$ for 30 steps, then upsamples, re-noises, and repeats the last 17 and 10 steps of the same schedule at $2048^2$ and $4096^2$. Each later stage pulls the estimate's low frequencies toward the previous stage. At those two stages HRDiT changes attention in four ways: NTK-scaled RoPE, a proportional attention scale, per-head key pruning, and position alignment. Progressive \sca{} keeps HRDiT's schedule, transitions, guidance, and seeds, and replaces all four changes with \sca{}. It uses native RoPE with the scale-2 table at $2048^2$ and the scale-4 table at $4096^2$. The stages re-enter at $\sigma=0.81$ and $0.61$, below $\sigma_g=0.89$, so the opening phase never runs; the $1024^2$ stage sets the layout. All settings were fixed before any evaluation image was generated (Appendix~\ref{app:additional_protocols}).

\section{What the heads want}
\label{sec:analysis}

We calibrate FLUX.1-dev on 32 purpose-written prompts at $1024^2$ and 16 at $1280^2$, disjoint from the evaluation sets. Errors are relative, $\sum J/\sum\|\Ofull\|^2$, pooled over blocks, heads, and steps.

\paragraph{Heads differ.} Over the 1368 heads, the standard deviation of $\pih$ is 0.24 to 0.26 at every noise level (emulated scale 2). Half of the (head, noise level) cells lie in $[0.2,0.8]$, 11.7\% exceed 0.95, and 2.9\% are below 0.05 (Figure~\ref{fig:method}b; full tables in Figure~\ref{fig:calib}, Appendix~\ref{app:calib}). The mean weight is 0.64 to 0.74, so the typical head leans local but still draws on the global branch.

\paragraph{The table is stable across tested prompt splits.} Tables fitted on disjoint halves of the prompts correlate at 0.989 over all cells (0.986 at scale 4; 0.974 for 16 prompts at $1280^2$). Each half's table, scored on the other half, gives a relative error of 0.0576 against 0.0574 in sample. Tables from 4, 8, or 16 prompts also generate similarly (Section~\ref{sec:generality}).

\paragraph{The weights transfer across the tested scales and resolutions.} The scale-2 table applied at scale 4 gives a relative error of 0.205 against 0.200 for the scale-4 table, still below every shared rule fitted at scale 4. The $1024^2$ table scored at $1280^2$ gives 0.0589 against 0.0585. Over all pairs of calibration targets, a transferred table adds at most 5.45\% error, while each target's own per-layer fit adds 12 to 24\%. The two resolutions emulate scale 2 with windows of 32 and 40 tokens, so this transfer is consistent with weights that depend mainly on the image-to-window ratio.

\paragraph{Per-head beats coarser rules; output error misses layout.} Relative to the best hard switch, per-head mixing lowers the relative error from 0.092 to 0.057 at scale 2 and from 0.261 to 0.200 at scale 4. Per-layer tables reach 0.071 and 0.223, and one shared weight per noise level 0.077 and 0.230 (Figure~\ref{fig:relerr}). In output energy the best hard switch never switches to global (Appendix~\ref{app:calib}), so the opening phase deliberately overrides the fit (Argument~\ref{arg:freq}).

\paragraph{Data.} The main prompts are the Aesthetic-4K evaluation split with 4K references~\citep{zhang2025diffusion4k}: 191 prompts after excluding four development prompts. A fixed 46-prompt subset serves the ablations and the panel comparison, and HRGen-1K~\citep{rajabi2026area} adds 200 prompts without references. Sizes, written height$\times$width, are $4096^2$, $2048\times4096$, and $2048\times6144$ (191 prompts each), $6144^2$ (100 prompts), and $8192^2$ (12 exploratory prompts). Every prompt uses seed label 0; seeds 1 and 2 repeat the 46-prompt subset.

\paragraph{Models.} FLUX.1-dev~\citep{flux2024} is the main model. FLUX.2-dev (50 prompts, against full attention) and FLUX.1-Krea-dev (100 prompts, against AReA) test other checkpoints, each calibrated separately on 16 prompts at $1024^2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:additional_protocols}).

\paragraph{Baselines.} Full attention, AReA from its released code (Appendix~\ref{app:area}), DyPE~\citep{issachar2025dype}, and SEGA~\citep{rajabi2026sega} run on all 191 prompts, and SigMa~\citep{zhao2026sigma} on the 46-prompt subset. They share AReA's sampler: 28 Euler flow-matching steps, guidance 4.5, shift $\mu=1.15$ at every size, bf16, and tiled VAE decoding. This fixes the sampler rather than each baseline's tuned defaults (Appendix~\ref{app:additional_protocols}). UltraImage~\citep{zhao2025ultraimage} runs its released guided two-stage recipe (30 steps per stage, guidance 3.5 then 7). HRDiT~\citep{xue2026hrdit} runs its progressive recipe (30, 17, and 10 steps at $1024^2$, $2048^2$, and $4096^2$). Both run on all 191 prompts as system-level comparisons with their own budgets.

\paragraph{Evaluation.} Preference and alignment scores are ImageReward, HPSv2 and HPSv2.1, PickScore, CLIP score, and aesthetic score~\citep{xu2023imagereward,wu2023hpsv2,kirstain2023pickapic,radford2021clip,schuhmann2022aesthetic}. Distribution scores are FID, KID, patch FID and KID, and Inception score~\citep{heusel2017fid,salimans2016improved}. No-reference quality uses MUSIQ on the full image and on $1024^2$ crops (MUSIQ$_\text{c}$), CLIP-IQA, and ARNIQA~\citep{ke2021musiq,wang2022clipiqa,agnolucci2024arniqa}. Intervals are 95\% paired bootstrap intervals over prompts; Appendix~\ref{app:metrics} defines every metric.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters.} \sca{} uses tile side $t=8$, window $w=64$, gate $\sigma_g=0.89$, and the scale-4 table from 32 prompts at $1024^2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:hparams}). The window equals the native grid by construction, and the gate reproduces AReA's 8 global steps of 28. The opening phase was tuned on development prompts only, and all progressive settings were fixed before any evaluation image was generated.

\paragraph{Compute.} Each process uses one NVIDIA GH200 120GB. At $4096^2$ and batch 1, \sca{} takes 71.1\,s per image, AReA 202.4\,s, and full attention 186.5\,s (median of three images). Main comparisons, ablations, and non-square sizes span over 2000 images. Surviving worker logs give a lower bound of 123.3 GH200-hours, including calibration and evaluation (Appendix~\ref{app:compute}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Training-free high-resolution sampling.} Training-free methods alter convolutions, spectra, or attention temperature~\citep{he2023scalecrafter,zhang2023hidiffusion,huang2024fouriscale,jin2023training}. Others fuse global and patch paths~\citep{du2023demofusion,yang2024fam,qiu2024freescale} or add native-resolution guidance~\citep{du2024imax,bu2025hiflow,koh2025scalediff}. Several adapt RoPE~\citep{zhuo2024lumina,issachar2025dype,zhao2026sigma,rajabi2026sega,liu2026tide}. Closest to \sca{}, AReA shares one schedule of pooled global attention and shrinking windows across heads~\citep{rajabi2026area}. ResDiT spectrally fuses position-scaled global and native-size patch attention between global-only and local-only phases~\citep{ma2025resdit}, and FreeScale fuses attention outputs by frequency~\citep{qiu2024freescale}.

\paragraph{Head- and layer-specific attention.} HRDiT picks per-head scopes by loss sensitivity under a cost budget~\citep{xue2026hrdit}, and UltraImage concentrates attention per head by entropy, noting stable local/global head roles before us~\citep{zhao2025ultraimage}. Sparse attention in diffusion transformers varies windows, spans, or key scales across layers or heads~\citep{yuan2024ditfastattn,xi2025svg,zhang2025sta}. Language models~\citep{fu2024moa,xiao2024duoattention} and vision transformers~\citep{pan2022hilo} do the same. Our local branch is neighbourhood attention~\citep{hassani2022nat}, and ToDo attends to downsampled keys without rescaling coordinates~\citep{smith2024todo}. Head specialization, two branches, and a global opening phase thus all have precedents. \sca{} adds calibration by emulation: a closed-form per-head, per-noise-level mixture fitted at native resolution and transferred to an operator with native key counts.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:limits}
\paragraph{Limitations.} Multi-stage recipes keep higher learned preference scores than single-pass \sca{}, HRDiT at $1.5\times$ and UltraImage at over four times its time. Progressive \sca{} closes the gap to HRDiT but costs fidelity, with timings from different code bases. At $2048\times4096$ \sca{} has no resolved ImageReward or FID advantage over AReA and trails on aesthetic score, CLIP-IQA, and patch FID/KID. The 191-prompt comparisons use one seed; extra seeds, SigMa, and progressive \sca{} cover 46 prompts, and FLUX.2-dev 50 prompts against full attention only. On \mbox{FLUX.1-Krea-dev} the ImageReward gain over AReA is unresolved and patch FID is higher. SigMa's dev-matched profile gives lattice-degraded images, possibly from our port or profile, not the method. We did not run ResDiT (no verified official code) or UltraImage's direct-extrapolation variant, so we claim no advantage over either. Late-appearing small extra subjects, $8192^2$ grid artifacts, and one prompt's doubled limbs remain (Appendix~\ref{app:failures}). All metrics are automatic, low-sample FID is noisy, and intervals ignore multiple comparisons.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} How much each head relies on a neighbourhood fixed in tokens versus a view of the whole image can be measured once at the training resolution and reused at 4K. With that table and a global-only opening, \sca{} is the strongest and fastest single-pass training-free operator we tested for FLUX.1-dev at $4096^2$, at about a third of AReA's time. Multi-stage recipes lead on learned preference metrics through their schedules. As a drop-in operator in HRDiT's progressive schedule, \sca{} matches HRDiT's preference scores in less than half its time, with no per-image metric significantly worse. Code: \codeurl.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\begin{lemma}[Softmax split]
\label{lem:split}
Let the keys of a query be partitioned into a near set $\Kset^{\mathrm{near}}$ and a far set $\Kset^{\mathrm{far}}$, with logits $\ell_j$. Then $\Ofull=m\,O^{\mathrm{near}}+(1-m)\,O^{\mathrm{far}}$ with $m=\sum_{j\in\Kset^{\mathrm{near}}}e^{\ell_j}/\sum_{j}e^{\ell_j}$ and $O^{\mathrm{near}},O^{\mathrm{far}}$ the softmax outputs restricted to $\Kset^{\mathrm{near}}$ and $\Kset^{\mathrm{far}}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $Z^{\mathrm{near}}=\sum_{j\in\Kset^{\mathrm{near}}}e^{\ell_j}$, so that $Z^{\mathrm{near}}O^{\mathrm{near}}=\sum_{j\in\Kset^{\mathrm{near}}}e^{\ell_j}v_j$, and likewise for the far set. Then $\Ofull=(Z^{\mathrm{near}}O^{\mathrm{near}}+Z^{\mathrm{far}}O^{\mathrm{far}})/(Z^{\mathrm{near}}+Z^{\mathrm{far}})$, and $Z^{\mathrm{near}}/(Z^{\mathrm{near}}+Z^{\mathrm{far}})=m$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:range}]
\emph{Global operator.} Image queries sit at $c(p_i)\in\Gnat=[n_0]^2$ and text queries at $0\in\Gnat$; subsampled keys sit at $b\in\Gnat$ and text keys at $0$. Any two points of $[n_0]^2$ differ by at most $n_0-1$ per axis. The key count is $T+|\Gnat|=T+n_0^2$.

\emph{Local operator.} Fix one axis of length $H\ge w$ and a tile $[t\tau,\,t\tau+t)$ with $t\le w$. The unclamped start $a'=t\tau+\lfloor t/2\rfloor-\lfloor w/2\rfloor$ satisfies $a'\le t\tau$ because $\lfloor t/2\rfloor\le\lfloor w/2\rfloor$, and $a'+w\ge t\tau+t$ because $w-\lfloor w/2\rfloor=\lceil w/2\rceil\ge\lceil t/2\rceil=t-\lfloor t/2\rfloor$. If $a'<0$ the window is $[0,w)$, and $w\ge a'+w\ge t\tau+t$. If $a'>H-w$ the window is $[H-w,H)$, with $H-w<a'\le t\tau$ and $H\ge t\tau+t$. In every case the window contains the tile, so it contains each query of the tile, and two positions in a window of side $w\le n_0$ differ by at most $n_0-1$. The key count is $T+w^2=T+n_0^2$ when $n_0\le\min(H,W)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Text-image offsets]
\label{rem:text}
FLUX.1 places all text tokens at position 0, so the offset between an image query at $p_i$ and a text key is $p_i$. In the global operator the query sits at $c(p_i)\in[n_0]^2$, inside the trained range. In the local operator it keeps $p_i$, up to $sn_0-1$ per axis, exactly as in full attention and AReA's windows at the target. Mapping text offsets through $c(\cdot)$ in the local operator is a possible refinement that we have not tested.
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:cost}]
Full attention scores every query against every key, $(N+T)^2$ pairs. The global operator scores $N+T$ queries against $T+|\Gnat|$ keys with $|\Gnat|=N/(s_Hs_W)=n_0^2$. The local operator scores $N$ image queries against $T$ text keys and $w^2$ window keys. The sum is at most $(2N+T)(T+w^2+N_0)$, which is $O(N(T+w^2+N_0))$ for fixed $n_0,T$. Value aggregation has the same pair counts. In the opening phase the local term is absent. The FlexAttention kernel visits whole blocks and masks the extra pairs: a query block of $8\times16$ tokens spans two tiles whose windows are 8 columns apart, so it visits a $64\times72$ key region, 12.5\% more than one window, plus partial blocks at clamped borders. This changes the constant, not the order.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:opt}]
$J(\pi)=E-2\pi B+\pi^2A$ with $A\ge0$. If $A=0$ then $\Oloc=\Oglob$ on every summand, so $B=0$ and $J$ is constant; any $\pi$, in particular $\pi^\star=1$, is optimal and the bound holds trivially. If $A>0$, $J$ is a strictly convex quadratic with unconstrained minimizer $B/A$, and its minimizer on $[0,1]$ is the projection $\clip(B/A,0,1)$. For any $\pi'$,
\[
J(\pi')-J(\pi^\star)=A(\pi'-\pi^\star)^2+2(\pi'-\pi^\star)(A\pi^\star-B).
\]
If $0<\pi^\star<1$ then $A\pi^\star=B$ and the second term vanishes. If $\pi^\star=0$ then $B\le0$, so $A\pi^\star-B\ge0$ and $\pi'-\pi^\star\ge0$. If $\pi^\star=1$ then $B\ge A$, so $A\pi^\star-B\le0$ and $\pi'-\pi^\star\le0$. In both clipped cases the second term is nonnegative.

Any rule that assigns one weight per cell, such as a per-layer table, a weight shared per noise level, a hard switch, or a table fitted elsewhere, is a feasible $\pi'$ in every cell, so its total error is at least the per-head table's, by $\sum_{\text{cells}}A(\pi'-\pi^\star)^2$ or more. A table interpolated in $\sigma$ between the mean sampled noise levels of occupied bins is not constant within a bin; the statement applies to the bin-constant table used to score rules in Section~\ref{sec:analysis}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The frequency argument in more detail.} Argument~\ref{arg:freq} rests on two facts and one assumption. The first fact is that the noisy latent is a sum of signal and independent white noise, so a band's signal-to-noise ratio scales as $((1-\sigma)/\sigma)^2S(f)$; at $\sigma=0.89$ the factor is $0.015$, and at $\sigma=0.5$ it is 1. The second is that a window of $w$ tokens cannot distinguish a component of period longer than $w$ from a smooth trend within the window, so windows cannot coordinate a layout at that scale except through what earlier layers mixed into their keys. The assumption is that natural-image latent spectra fall with frequency, which we have not measured for FLUX.1 latents. Together they predict that windowed attention fails first and worst at high noise and for objects larger than the window, which matches the duplication we observe (Figure~\ref{fig:failure}) and the effect of a 16-token window.

\paragraph{Statistics.} For every emulated scale, block, head, and noise bin, the pass stores $A$, $B$, $E$, the error of the local branch alone $\sum\|\Ofull-\Oloc\|^2$, $\sum\|\Ofull\|^2$, and the query count, in float64, summed over image queries and channels (\texttt{sca/calib.py}). Text-query outputs are not in the objective, because \sca{} sends text queries through the global branch only.

\paragraph{FlexAttention window.} Image tokens are reordered tile-major, so that a sparse block is a small spatial tile. Query blocks cover $8\times16$ tokens (128 queries) and key blocks $8\times8$ (64 keys), shifted so that unclamped windows start on a block edge; text keys come first. The mask function states window membership per query-key pair, so the result does not depend on the block layout, only the amount of skipped work does. On GH200, $8\times16$ query blocks with $8\times8$ key blocks were fastest at $2048^2$, $4096^2$, and $2048\times6144$. RoPE, subsampling, both branches, and the mix compile into one graph per layer; the mix is in fp32.

\paragraph{Numerical checks.} The 18 CPU tests in \texttt{tests/test\_sca.py} use float32 to check full-mode agreement with stock FLUX attention, both branches against brute-force Equations~\ref{eq:glob} and~\ref{eq:loc}, full-grid and factor-1 identities, calibration when the local branch is exact, the gate, and FlexAttention against an explicit gather reference. A separate GPU check in \texttt{scripts/bench\_attn.py} compares the gather and FlexAttention implementations in bf16: the recorded maximum absolute difference is 0.015625, and both have the same maximum error against the fp32 reference on the tested grids. This is an observed absolute-error check; no per-element ULP bound was measured.

\paragraph{Position ids in bf16.} The FLUX.1 pipeline builds image position ids in bf16, which cannot represent every integer above 256. Our frozen runs retain these rounded ids for RoPE. Passing the true grid from the sampler fixes window membership and subsampling geometry, but does not restore exact RoPE coordinates: the coarse-query positions are also computed from the rounded ids. At a 384-token axis, index 383 rounds to 384 and its scale-6 coarse coordinate becomes 64; at a 512-token axis, index 511 rounds to 512 and its scale-8 coarse coordinate also becomes 64. A local 64-token window can likewise have a rounded endpoint difference of 64 instead of the ideal bound 63. Proposition~\ref{prop:range} therefore applies exactly to the mathematical operator, not to these finite-precision positions at $2048\times6144$, $6144^2$, or $8192^2$. The $4096^2$ grid uses indices 0--255, which bf16 represents exactly. FLUX.2 uses integer position ids. Rebuilding FLUX.1 positions in higher precision would require a separate controlled experiment; the reported runs are unchanged.

\paragraph{Non-divisible grids.} When $H/n_0$ is not an integer, the subsampling factor per axis is the divisor of $H$ nearest to $H/n_0$, and the tile side the divisor nearest to $t$; neither case arises at the sizes we evaluate.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=scacode, title={\texttt{sca/attn.py}: SCAProcessor.sca}, emph={sca,geometry,_split,coarse_rope,rope,global_branch,pi_now,flex_layout,sca_flex_fn}]
def sca(self, q, k, v, image_rotary_emb):
    ctx = self.ctx
    f, tile, win = ctx.geometry()      # f = (H/n0, W/n0), t = 8, w = min(n0, H) per axis
    if ctx.gate_sigma > 0 and ctx.sigma is not None and float(ctx.sigma) > ctx.gate_sigma:
        L, rope_txt = self._split(q, image_rotary_emb)
        cos, sin = image_rotary_emb
        rq, rk = ctx.coarse_rope(f, q.device)
        kr_txt = rope(k[:, :L], rope_txt)
        return global_branch(q, k, v, L, ctx.grid, f, ctx.pool, rope_txt, rq, rk,
                             rope(q[:, :L], rope_txt), kr_txt)  # opening phase, Eq. (*@\ref{eq:mix}@*)
    pi = ctx.pi_now(self.layer, q.shape[2], q.device).view(1, 1, -1, 1)  # (*@$\pi_{lh}(\sigma)$@*), per head
    # ... route > 0 (hard per-head routing) and band (App. (*@\ref{app:band}@*)): variants, off by default
    if ctx.local_impl == "flex" and ctx.fused:
        L, _ = self._split(q, image_rotary_emb)
        rq, rk = ctx.coarse_rope(f, q.device)
        layout = ctx.flex_layout(L, tile, win, q.device)    # BlockMask, cached per layout
        return sca_flex_fn(q.device)(q, k, v, tuple(image_rotary_emb), rq, rk, pi, L,
                                     ctx.grid, f, ctx.pool, layout)
    # ... unfused path: the same arithmetic through self.branches() and mix_branches()
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=scacode, title={\texttt{sca/attn.py}: sca\_flex\_forward}, emph={sca_flex_forward,rope,global_branch,local_branch_flex,mix_branches}]
def sca_flex_forward(q, k, v, rot, rope_q, rope_k, pi, L, grid, f, pool, layout):
    cos, sin = rot                        # q, k, v: pre-RoPE [B, S, h, D], text first
    kr = rope(k, rot)                                   # all keys at their own positions
    qi = rope(q[:, L:], (cos[L:], sin[L:]))             # image queries at p_i
    q_txt = rope(q[:, :L], (cos[:L], sin[:L]))          # text queries (FLUX.1: position 0)
    o_glob = global_branch(q, k, v, L, grid, f, pool, (cos[:L], sin[:L]), rope_q, rope_k,
                           q_txt, kr[:, :L])                                    # Eq. (*@\ref{eq:glob}@*)
    o_loc = local_branch_flex(qi, kr, v, L, layout, permuted=True)            # Eq. (*@\ref{eq:loc}@*)
    return mix_branches(o_glob, o_loc, pi, L, layout.q_inv)                    # Eq. (*@\ref{eq:mix}@*)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=scacode, title={\texttt{sca/attn.py}: global\_branch}, emph={global_branch,pool_tokens,rope,sdpa}]
def global_branch(q, k, v, L, grid, f, pool, rope_txt, rope_q, rope_k,
                  q_txt=None, k_txt=None):                                     # Eq. (*@\ref{eq:glob}@*)
    kp = pool_tokens(k[:, L:], grid, f, pool)            # block-centre keys (*@$k_{\kappa(b)}$@*)
    vp = pool_tokens(v[:, L:], grid, f, pool)
    q_txt = rope(q[:, :L], rope_txt) if q_txt is None else q_txt
    k_txt = rope(k[:, :L], rope_txt) if k_txt is None else k_txt
    qr = torch.cat([q_txt, rope(q[:, L:], rope_q)], 1)   # image queries at (*@$c(p_i)$@*)
    kr = torch.cat([k_txt, rope(kp, rope_k)], 1)         # pooled keys at block index (*@$b$@*)
    return sdpa(qr, kr, torch.cat([v[:, :L], vp], 1))    # every query, text and image
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=scacode, title={\texttt{sca/attn.py}: pool\_tokens}, emph={pool_tokens}]
def pool_tokens(x, grid, f, pool):                       # x: image tokens [B, H*W, h, D]
    B, N, h, D = x.shape
    (H, W), (fh, fw) = grid, f
    if fh == fw == 1:
        return x
    g = x.view(B, H, W, h, D)
    if pool == "center":                 # token (*@$\kappa(b)$@*) of every (*@$f_h\times f_w$@*) block, Eq. (*@\ref{eq:glob}@*)
        g = g[:, fh // 2::fh, fw // 2::fw]
    # ... pool == "mean" averages each block instead (the mean-pooling ablation)
    return g.reshape(B, -1, h, D)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=scacode, title={\texttt{sca/attn.py}: SCAContext.coarse\_rope}, emph={coarse_rope,pos_embed}]
def coarse_rope(self, f, device):                        # returns (rope_q, rope_k)
    key = (f, str(device))
    if key not in self._rope_cache:
        ids = self._img_ids.float()                      # FLUX ids (0, row, col), [N, 3]
        div = torch.tensor([1.0, f[0], f[1]])
        q_ids = torch.cat([ids[:, :1],                   # queries at (*@$c(p)=\lfloor p/f\rfloor$@*), Eq. (*@\ref{eq:glob}@*)
                           torch.floor((ids[:, 1:] - ids[:, 1:].amin(0)) / div[1:])], 1)
        H, W = self.grid                                 # keys at (*@$c(\kappa(b))=b$@*):
        k_ids = q_ids.view(H, W, 3)[f[0] // 2::f[0], f[1] // 2::f[1]].reshape(-1, 3)
        self._rope_cache[key] = (self.pos_embed(q_ids), self.pos_embed(k_ids))
    return self._rope_cache[key]
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=scacode, title={\texttt{sca/attn.py}: local\_branch\_flex}, emph={local_branch_flex,flex_fn}]
def local_branch_flex(qi, kr, v, L, layout, permuted=False):                  # Eq. (*@\ref{eq:loc}@*)
    qp = qi.index_select(1, layout.q_gather)                  # image queries, tile-major
    kp = torch.cat([kr[:, :L], kr[:, L:].index_select(1, layout.k_gather)], 1)
    vp = torch.cat([v[:, :L], v[:, L:].index_select(1, layout.k_gather)], 1)  # text first
    kw = {"kernel_options": layout.kernel_options} if layout.kernel_options else {}
    o = flex_fn(qi.device)(qp.transpose(1, 2), kp.transpose(1, 2), vp.transpose(1, 2),
                           block_mask=layout.block_mask, **kw)    # one FlexAttention call
    return o if permuted else o.transpose(1, 2).index_select(1, layout.q_inv)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=scacode, title={\texttt{sca/attn.py}: flex\_layout}, emph={flex_layout,tile_order,mask_mod,create_block_mask,FlexLayout}]
def flex_layout(L, grid, tile, window, device, q_tile=None, k_tile=None, kernel_options=None):
    # ... imports and torch.compile set-up
    (H, W), (th, tw), (Wh, Ww) = grid, tile, window
    q_tile, k_tile = q_tile or FLEX_Q_TILE, k_tile or FLEX_K_TILE    # (8, 16), (8, 8)
    # window start (*@$a(\tau)$@*) of each query's tile, per row and column, Eq. (*@\ref{eq:loc}@*)
    r_start = (torch.arange(H) // th * th + th // 2 - Wh // 2).clamp(0, H - Wh)
    c_start = (torch.arange(W) // tw * tw + tw // 2 - Ww // 2).clamp(0, W - Ww)
    k_off = ((Wh // 2 - th // 2) % k_tile[0], (Ww // 2 - tw // 2) % k_tile[1])
    qo = tile_order(grid, q_tile)             # tile-major slot -> image index, -1 = pad
    ko = tile_order(grid, k_tile, k_off)      # key tiles shifted so windows start on a tile
    q_gather, k_gather = qo.clamp(min=0), ko.clamp(min=0)
    # ... q_inv: inverse query permutation (image index -> slot), used to un-permute O_loc
    qr0 = r_start[q_gather // W]              # window corner of every query slot
    qc0 = c_start[q_gather % W]
    far = -(1 << 30)
    krow = torch.where(ko >= 0, k_gather // W, far)     # row and column of every key slot
    kcol = torch.where(ko >= 0, k_gather % W, far)
    def mask_mod(b, h, q, kv):                # kv in (*@$\mathcal{K}^{\mathrm{loc}}_q$@*): text key, or in q's window
        j = (kv - L).clamp(min=0)
        r, c, r0, c0 = krow[j], kcol[j], qr0[q], qc0[q]
        return (kv < L) | ((r >= r0) & (r < r0 + Wh) & (c >= c0) & (c < c0 + Ww))
    nq, nk = qo.numel(), L + ko.numel()
    bs = (q_tile[0] * q_tile[1], k_tile[0] * k_tile[1])  # blocks of 128 queries, 64 keys
    # ... static shapes for dynamo; _compile=True on CUDA
    bm = create_block_mask(mask_mod, None, None, nq, nk, BLOCK_SIZE=bs)
    # ... ko = kernel options for flex_attention (BLOCK_M, BLOCK_N, USE_TMA)
    return FlexLayout(q_gather, q_inv, k_gather, bm, ko)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=scacode, title={\texttt{sca/attn.py}: mix\_branches}, emph={mix_branches}]
def mix_branches(o_glob, o_loc, pi, L, inv=None):
    if inv is not None:                                     # un-permute the flex output
        o_loc = o_loc.transpose(1, 2).index_select(1, inv)
    img = (pi * o_loc.float() + (1 - pi) * o_glob[:, L:].float()).to(o_glob.dtype)  # Eq. (*@\ref{eq:mix}@*)
    return torch.cat([o_glob[:, :L], img], 1)               # text queries keep O_glob
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=scacode, title={\texttt{sca/attn.py}: SCAProcessor.calibrate}, emph={calibrate,rope,sdpa,geometry,branches,add}]
def calibrate(self, q, k, v, image_rotary_emb):
    ctx = self.ctx
    qr, kr = rope(q, image_rotary_emb), rope(k, image_rotary_emb)
    full = sdpa(qr, kr, v)                   # O_full, returned: the sampler runs unmodified
    for i, s in enumerate(ctx.calib_scales):                   # emulated scales s_c = 2, 4
        f, tile, win = ctx.geometry(s)       # f = s_c, w = n / s_c, t = round(8 w / n0)
        L, o_loc, o_glob, _ = self.branches(q, k, v, image_rotary_emb, f, tile, win, qr, kr)
        ctx.calib.add(self.layer, i, ctx.sigma, full[:, L:], o_loc, o_glob[:, L:])  # Eq. (*@\ref{eq:stats}@*)
    # ... add_mass(): attention-mass diagnostics on 256 sampled queries
    return full
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=scacode, title={\texttt{sca/calib.py}: CalibStats.add}, emph={add,bin}]
@torch.no_grad()
def add(self, layer, i, sigma, full, loc, glob):     # image rows only, [B, N, h, D]
    f, l, g = full.float(), loc.float(), glob.float()
    dl, df = l - g, f - g
    red = lambda x: x.sum((0, 1, 3)).double()        # per head (sum over B, N, D)
    n = torch.full_like(red(f[..., :1]), f.shape[0] * f.shape[1])
    vals = torch.stack([red(dl * dl), red(df * dl), red(df * df),  # A, B, E of Eq. (*@\ref{eq:stats}@*)
                        red((f - l) ** 2), red(f * f), n])        # E_fl, F2, count
    self.stats[i, :, layer, :, self.bin(sigma)] += vals   # bin = min(int(14 sigma), 13)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=scacode, title={\texttt{sca/calib.py}: \_ratio}, emph={_ratio}]
def _ratio(b, a):                           # Prop. (*@\ref{prop:opt}@*): clip(B / A, 0, 1), and 1 where A = 0
    return np.clip(np.where(a > 0, b / np.where(a > 0, a, 1.0), 1.0), 0.0, 1.0)
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Prompts and references.} The Aesthetic-4K evaluation split~\citep{zhang2025diffusion4k} has 195 prompts with 4K reference images. Four (boats, Moai, woman and bear, underwater surfer) were used to choose the opening phase and window and are excluded from generation evaluation, leaving 191. Distribution metrics retain all 195 official reference rows. The 46-prompt ablation subset was fixed before any ablation was run. HRGen-1K~\citep{rajabi2026area} has 1000 prompts and no references; we use source rows 0--199, with ids \texttt{hrgen\_0000}--\texttt{hrgen\_0199}. The 48 calibration prompts were written for this purpose and share no prompt with Aesthetic-4K (the largest word-set Jaccard overlap with any evaluation prompt is 0.26).

\paragraph{Preference and alignment metrics.} ImageReward v1.0~\citep{xu2023imagereward} (bicubic resize to 224, centre crop). HPSv2 and HPSv2.1~\citep{wu2023hpsv2}: cosine of normalized image and text features with the official validation transform (longest side 224, padded). PickScore~\citep{kirstain2023pickapic}: $\exp(\text{logit scale})$ times the cosine. CLIP score~\citep{radford2021clip}: $100\max(\cos,0)$ with ViT-L/14. Aesthetic: the LAION improved aesthetic predictor (sac+logos+ava1, linear MSE) on the L2-normalized CLIP ViT-L/14 image embedding~\citep{schuhmann2022aesthetic}.

\paragraph{No-reference quality.} MUSIQ~\citep{ke2021musiq} (pyiqa defaults) on the full image at native resolution, and MUSIQ$_\text{c}$, its mean over four fixed random $1024^2$ crops per image; crop positions depend only on the prompt id, so they are identical across methods. CLIP-IQA~\citep{wang2022clipiqa} and ARNIQA~\citep{agnolucci2024arniqa} (pyiqa defaults) on the image downscaled with antialiased bicubic resampling to a long side of 1024 pixels, the scale both were trained on.

\paragraph{Distribution metrics and reference correction.} FID~\citep{heusel2017fid} and KID compare the generated images (191, or 46 on the subset) with all 195 official reference rows, using Inception-v3 features (torch-fidelity) after bilinear resizing to $299^2$. The reference rows contain 168 distinct image contents; repeated rows retain their official weights. Patch metrics use four $1024^2$ crops per row (764 generated and 780 reference crops). An audit found that the original feature-bank assembly keyed reference crops by resolved image path, so repeated images reused the last row's crop features. We corrected the lookup to retain each row's intended cached crops. Whole-image FID/KID are unchanged; all patch point estimates reported here use the corrected assembly. Original CSVs remain archived for provenance. Inception score uses full images at $299^2$, with 10 splits.

\paragraph{Intervals.} Per-image intervals are 95\% paired percentile bootstraps over prompt ids: original summaries use 1000 draws; separately recomputed comparisons use 2000 draws with seed 0. For the main \sca{}--AReA FID/KID and patch-FID/KID comparison, 1000 draws resample generated prompt ids jointly across methods and resample the 168 distinct reference-image identities, keeping all rows and crops from each sampled identity together. This retains the 195-row weighted point estimate while accounting for repeated reference images. The resulting FID difference is $-7.63$ $[-12.79,-2.94]$; patch FID is $-3.60$ $[-7.39,1.15]$ and patch KID ($\times10^3$) is $-2.27$ $[-4.73,0.12]$. An earlier version of this analysis reported a significant patch-KID improvement; under the corrected evaluation its interval includes zero. The whole-image and patch FID/KID comparisons at both non-square sizes and on the 46-prompt panel, the added baselines' patch comparisons, and the 56-step patch-FID comparison also use reference-image clusters. Other whole-image FID/KID intervals are labelled historical reference-row bootstraps; they have not been recomputed with image-identity clusters. Historical patch intervals are not attached to corrected estimates and are omitted where recomputation is incomplete. FID's finite-sample bias can place point estimates outside percentile intervals. Intervals are unadjusted for multiple comparisons and measure prompt/reference sampling uncertainty, not variability over independent model training runs.

\section{Reproducing AReA}
\label{app:area}

We run AReA's released code with its FLUX.1 defaults (block-centre pooling, first phase for 8 of 28 steps, halo width 8, bridge ratio 0.5), the FLUX.1-dev checkpoint, guidance 4.5, 28 steps, $\mu$ at its maximum, and the seeds shared with the other methods. AReA's paper reports ImageReward 1.32 at 4K under its own settings; our shared protocol gives 0.92, so cross-paper numbers are not comparable, while our direct \sca{}--AReA comparison holds these settings fixed. UltraImage and FLUX.2-dev use separate protocols (Appendix~\ref{app:additional_protocols}). AReA's paper reports 151\,s for AReA and 314\,s for full attention at $4096^2$ on an H100; the matched sweep measures 202.4\,s and 186.5\,s on one GH200 (medians of three images). We use commit \texttt{9e38ec2} of the released repository, which includes a cost cap on halo and bridge tokens and a jagged halo edge; neither appears in AReA's paper, which does not say whether its Table~1 used them. Recomputed from the code with 512 text tokens, the first-phase pooling factors are 4, 4, 4, 4, 2, 2, 2, 2 at $4096\times4096$ and $2048\times4096$, and 8, 8, 8, 4, 4, 4, 2, 2 at $2048\times6144$. The second-phase windows shrink over 20 steps from 180, 151, and 192 tokens respectively to 64. At $2048\times6144$ no window is cheaper than full attention once the text tokens are counted, so the first window falls back to 192 tokens and the cap drops the bridge and halo on the first three second-phase steps. Other differences from the release defaults are the FLUX.1-dev checkpoint instead of FLUX.1-Krea-dev, and one seed per prompt shared across methods instead of one seed for all prompts. On FLUX.1-Krea-dev, AReA's default checkpoint, we rerun AReA and recalibrate \sca{} (16 prompts at $1024^2$, scale-4 table, other settings unchanged) with the same 28-step, guidance-4.5 sampler on 100 prompts; DyPE and SEGA run the Krea transformer through the frozen baseline harness. There \sca{} matches AReA at a third of its time (Table~\ref{tab:krea_6144}).

\section{Other baselines and FLUX.2-dev}
\label{app:additional_protocols}

\paragraph{DyPE and SEGA.} We use the existing baseline harness frozen at commit \texttt{c05ebdc}, with FLUX.1-dev, 28 steps, guidance 4.5, maximum shift, tiled VAE decoding, and the same prompt-derived seeds as \sca{}. These settings isolate the attention/position treatment on the checkpoint used in our main comparison. They do not reproduce the methods' reported FLUX.1-Krea-dev results; on Krea weights both trail AReA on ImageReward (Table~\ref{tab:krea_6144}). Their low FLUX.1-dev scores should therefore not be read as a ranking under each method's preferred checkpoint.

\paragraph{UltraImage.} We run a guided two-stage configuration from the FLUX branch of the released implementation through \texttt{scripts/run\_ultraimage.py}. It uses a $1024^2$ first stage and a $4096^2$ second stage, 30 steps per stage, guidance 3.5 then 7, the \texttt{super\_imax} guidance schedule, shifted-window patchification, proportional attention, RIFLEx positions, and attention decay. Each prompt also needs a 2+2-step block-average preparation pass. The reported campaign time includes preparation when not cached and the two-stage call; main-stage and preparation times are retained separately in sidecars. Each call reseeds both the generator and the global RNG, including the VAE sampling path, from the prompt id and seed label. Thus seed labels are paired, but the two-stage method does not share an identical latent trajectory with the one-stage methods. The wrapper preserves an upstream scheduler behaviour: assigning \texttt{scheduler.config.shift} does not update the scheduler's internal shift in the installed diffusers version, so both stages retain shift 3.0. This is a reproduction caveat, not a newly tuned setting. We report this system-level comparison separately from the common-sampler attention comparisons.

\paragraph{HRDiT.} We run the released progressive protocol of the official repository (commit \texttt{f3be935}) on FLUX.1-dev in fp16: 30 steps at $1024^2$ with guidance 3.5, then 17 steps at $2048^2$ with guidance 4.5 and 10 steps at $4096^2$ with guidance 6, 57 steps in all, with latent upsampling and the shipped per-head scope plan and attention settings. Its sampling budget therefore differs from the shared protocol, and we report it with the multi-stage recipes. HRDiT's global Torch random state is reset per prompt because its Gaussian blur draws a random width. Its clean time, 108.6\,s, is the median of three batch-1 runs on the latency prompts (the first, 144.4\,s, includes warm-up). The 191-prompt HRDiT evaluation used the original crop assembly, under which patch FID is 110.7 for HRDiT and 106.5 for \sca{}. Recomputed from the cached crop features with the corrected assembly of Table~\ref{tab:main} (\texttt{repro/\allowbreak evaluate\_corrected.py}), HRDiT's patch FID is 103.9, against 99.8 for \sca{} and 103.4 for AReA.

\paragraph{Progressive \sca{}.} \texttt{scripts/sample\_progressive.py} reimplements HRDiT's released schedule with the stock diffusers FLUX components and changes only the attention (Section~\ref{sec:progressive}). One 30-step schedule with dynamic shift $\mu=1.15$ (from the $1024^2$ sequence length) serves all stages, and the recorded HRDiT noise levels are reproduced to $10^{-6}$. The $2048^2$ stage repeats steps 14 to 30 and the $4096^2$ stage steps 21 to 30. Each transition decodes the latent, upsamples it bicubically, applies HRDiT's unsharp mask with a blur width drawn from the per-prompt global seed, re-encodes it, and re-noises it with the per-prompt generator; guidance is 3.5, 4.5, and 6.0, and HRDiT's low-band structure guidance and velocity momentum are kept. At $1024^2$ both methods use dense attention with native RoPE. At $2048^2$ and $4096^2$ we remove NTK RoPE, the proportional scale, head pruning, and position alignment, and use \sca{} with the FLUX.1-dev table at scales 2 and 4, $n_0=64$, $t=8$, and $\sigma_g=0.89$, which no high-resolution step exceeds. Deliberate differences from HRDiT's runner are bf16 instead of fp16 and a finite-value check at the end of each stage instead of after every step; with bf16 and stock components the $1024^2$ images, and hence the layouts, differ from HRDiT's for the same seed (Figure~\ref{fig:progressive}). Reported time is CUDA-synchronized and covers text encoding, all stages, transitions, and the final decode, excluding model loading; per-stage means are 5.1, 12.3, and 26.1\,s, peak memory 37.8\,GiB. The gate and every other setting were fixed before generation; two development prompts served as a smoke test.

\paragraph{SigMa.} The dev-matched profile runs the released code (commit \texttt{fbe3a86}) on FLUX.1-dev under the shared sampler: 28 steps, guidance 4.5, bf16, YaRN positions, and the shift capped at its maximum from the target sequence length. SigMa's paper states FLUX.1-dev with guidance 3.5, while its released command line hardcodes FLUX.1-Krea-dev with guidance 4.5; we ran only the profile that matches our protocol. The outputs are finite, fully decoded $4096^2$ images, but the images we inspected show a regular lattice of dots, as DyPE's do at $4096^2$ on FLUX.1-dev. This may reflect our port or profile rather than the method, so the SigMa row should not be read as the method's best result.

\paragraph{Adapter for HRDiT and SigMa.} SigMa runs on the 46-prompt subset and HRDiT on all 191 prompts, both through one adapter with campaign per-prompt CPU generators, resident models, and tiled VAE decoding. These differ from the upstream command-line seed, offload, and decode choices, so the runs are controlled adaptations, not reproductions of the authors' tables. Campaign time includes text encoding, decoding, and finite-value checks after every step, which add synchronization overhead; it excludes model loading and is not a clean latency benchmark.

\paragraph{FLUX.2-dev.} The model has 56 attention blocks and 48 heads per block. We reuse the two branches and calibration rule with a native grid of $64^2$ tokens, local window 64, and query tile 8. The four-axis positions preserve the time and text axes while coarsening the two spatial axes. Calibration uses 16 prompts at $1024^2$, 14 noise bins, and emulated scales 2 and 4; sampling uses the scale-4 table. Both full attention and \sca{} use 28 steps, guidance 4.0, seed label 0, and a shift fixed to the native sequence length, $\mu=2.1514$. The pipeline's default extrapolated shift at $4096^2$ would be about 11.55; we therefore do not interpret this comparison as a default-pipeline benchmark. The gate is 0.959 so that the first 8 steps use only global attention. The 50 evaluation prompts are the first 50 Aesthetic-4K prompts remaining after development-prompt exclusion. Prompt embeddings are cached for both methods; reported times include denoising and tiled VAE decoding, and exclude text encoding and model loading. At emulated scale 4, fitted per-head relative output error is 0.1790, versus 0.1889 for per-layer weights and 0.1924 for local-only attention; split-half correlation is 0.950 and cross-half error 0.1794. These are calibration diagnostics, not generation ablations of the individual components on FLUX.2.

\section{The low-band duplication guard}
\label{app:band}

\paragraph{Construction.} Let $\LP$ average-pool a head's image output over $p\times p$ tokens and upsample it bilinearly, and $\HP=\mathrm{I}-\LP$. The guard replaces the low band of the mix $M$ by that of the global branch:
\begin{equation}
  O \;=\; \LP\big(\Oglob\big) + \HP\big(M\big) \;=\; \Oglob + \pih\,\HP\big(\Oloc-\Oglob\big).
\end{equation}
The local branch then contributes only its difference from the global branch above the cutoff. The cutoff should lie below $1/(2s)$, where the global branch aliases, and above $1/w$, below which the window cannot resolve; with $w=64$ and $s=4$ this leaves periods of 8 to 64 tokens. We used $p=16$; $p=8$ and $p=32$ were worse on four development prompts, and a noise-dependent cutoff behaved like $p=16$. FreeScale~\citep{qiu2024freescale} also fuses two self-attention outputs by frequency band, in the opposite direction: it keeps the blurred low band of attention over overlapping crops and takes the high band from full self-attention at the target size. ResDiT also combines global low frequencies and local high frequencies, using patch-wise FFT masks~\citep{ma2025resdit}. Our optional guard uses pooled global attention and preserves the fitted per-head weight on the local-minus-global high-band residual. Its global-low/local-high construction is therefore not itself novel.

\paragraph{History: what it fixes and what it costs.} On the development prompts, the band alone (no opening phase) fixed the boats but left extra statues and faces on the Moai and woman-and-bear prompts; the opening phase alone got the main subject right, but small extra figures appeared late on some prompts; both together gave one subject on the boats, Moai, and woman-and-bear prompts. This was our first full configuration. On 46 prompts, however, removing the guard improves HPSv2 ($+0.54$), HPSv2.1 ($+1.17$), PickScore ($+0.25$), CLIP-IQA ($+1.7$), and MUSIQ ($+2.9$), all significant, leaves the other metrics within noise, and saves 17\,s per image on an idle GPU. The guard also hid what the per-head mix contributes. With it (Table~\ref{tab:ablation_band}), local only matched the per-head mix within 2\% on preference metrics, because the guard replaces the low band of every head's output with that of the global branch whatever the head's weight, so it injects global low frequencies even at $\pi=1$; without the guard local only loses on nearly every metric (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}). Likewise, with the guard one shared weight and per-layer weights matched the per-head table on preference metrics and lost only on no-reference quality, and hard per-head routing (10 to 17\% of heads global at threshold 0.5, about 6\% at 0.3) stayed within 2\% of the mix. The guard is an option for prompts where late duplication is visible, not part of the default method.

\section{Compute and reproducibility}
\label{app:compute}

\paragraph{Compute.} Generation and calibration used single NVIDIA GH200 GPUs on a shared cluster; the verified timing logs identify the GH200 120\,GB model. Some metric evaluations ran on CPUs. The surviving logs do not establish the complete calibration runtime. Generation at $4096^2$ takes about 71\,s per image for \sca{}, 262\,s for AReA, and 197\,s for full attention on shared nodes (campaign means); the main comparisons, ablations, and non-square sizes comprise more than 2000 generated images. Surviving worker logs establish a lower bound of 123.3 GH200 worker-hours through 2026-10-17 06:59 UTC, including model loading, calibration, evaluation, and failed attempts. The bound is the union of 581 closed task intervals per physical node, with timestamp precision treated conservatively; it measures occupied worker time, not GPU kernel activity. Missing historical logs, unmatched starts, and ongoing work prevent a complete campaign total. Overlapping scheduler allocation time and image-sidecar durations are not added to this bound.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} The code (attention processor, calibration, sampling, evaluation wrappers, AReA wrapper, and tests), the calibration table \texttt{k1\_1024.npz}, the per-image metric files, and the per-comparison summary and paired-delta CSV files behind every table will be released. Main-text metric values and paired intervals are derived from those CSV files. Each \sca{} generation task ran from a frozen copy of the code named in the task. Per-image sidecar files record the sampling settings and, where available, a code fingerprint; the inherited Krea baseline records do not establish one source revision for every image.

\section{Broader impact}
\label{app:impact}

\sca{} shortens generation at $4096^2$ relative to full attention under our measured protocol, which helps applications such as print, design, and scientific visualization. Energy use remains unmeasured. The same reduction lowers the cost of producing high-resolution synthetic images that could be used to mislead. \sca{} adds no capability beyond the base model's and does not change its safety filters or watermarking; the risks and mitigations of the base model apply unchanged.

\section{Failure cases}
\label{app:failures}

\snippet{failures}{}

Figure~\ref{fig:failures} shows the duplication that windows cause without the gate, and local errors that \sca{} still makes.

\paragraph{Late duplication.} With the final method, small extra figures can appear on some prompts in the last 20 steps, when the local branch dominates most heads. The band guard suppresses some of them but costs preference scores. \paragraph{Articulated subjects.} The underwater-surfer development prompt keeps doubled limbs in every variant; AReA's image shows a large translucent ghost. \paragraph{Small factors.} At $2048\times4096$ the window covers half the image height, so the local branch alone already sees most of the image along that axis, and the per-head mix gains little over AReA's shrinking windows; aesthetic score and corrected patch FID are significantly worse. \paragraph{Larger scales and another backbone.} The gallery sampled from the completed 12-prompt $8192^2$ run still shows grid and stripe artifacts in both methods (Figure~\ref{fig:gallery_8192_complete}); this exploratory set does not establish general 8K quality. In FLUX.2-dev, improved aggregate metrics coexist with distorted facial geometry and fine texture in the randomly selected portrait example (Figure~\ref{fig:gallery_flux2}). \paragraph{Text-image offsets.} The local branch keeps out-of-range text-image offsets (Remark~\ref{rem:text}). \paragraph{Metrics.} No-reference quality metrics reward sharp repeated subjects (v0 scores higher on CLIP-IQA and ARNIQA), and FID against 195 references at $299^2$ resolution sees little of the 4K detail; we therefore report every metric and the qualitative galleries.
