\begin{abstract}
Diffusion transformers sampled far beyond their training resolution need attention that is cheap and stays inside the training distribution. Recent training-free methods give every head the same hand-scheduled receptive field. We ask FLUX.1-dev, at its training resolution, how much of each head's output a native-size window explains and how much a downsampled view of the whole image explains. A per-head, per-noise-level convex mix of these two in-distribution branches fits full attention closely. Its weight is stable across prompts (split-half correlation 0.99) and transfers across emulated scales and calibration resolutions at almost no cost. Scale-Covariant Attention (\sca{}) reuses this table at the target resolution. For the first 8 of 28 steps, while only layout-scale structure carries signal, every head uses the global branch alone, whose keys are pooled onto the native grid with rescaled coordinates. Afterwards each head mixes the global branch with an exact native-extent sliding-window attention by its calibrated weight. \sca{} needs one calibration pass and no training, and its attention cost is linear in the token count. On 191 Aesthetic-4K prompts at $4096^2$, under one protocol shared with AReA's released code, \sca{} raises ImageReward from 0.92 to 1.23 and HPSv2.1 from 0.272 to 0.297, lowers FID from 146.0 to 138.4, and is significantly better on every preference, alignment, and no-reference quality metric we report. At 71\,s per image on one GH200 it is 2.6 times faster than full attention and 3.0 times faster than AReA. The advantage grows with the scale factor: \sca{} ties AReA at $2048\times4096$ and raises ImageReward from 0.39 to 0.66 at $2048\times6144$. Both parts of the method are needed: without the opening phase subjects duplicate, and replacing the per-head mix with local-only attention after it lowers ImageReward by 0.36, because the mix is what carries global context through the remaining steps.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Text-to-image diffusion transformers such as FLUX.1~\citep{flux2024} and Stable Diffusion~3~\citep{esser2024sd3} are trained at about one megapixel. Sampling them at $4096\times4096$ multiplies the number of image tokens by 16 and the cost of full attention by about 256. Speed is the smaller problem. Every attention layer then sees a token count and a range of relative positions outside its training distribution, and the outputs show duplicated subjects, broken layouts, and blurred texture~\citep{he2023scalecrafter,du2023demofusion,issachar2025dype,rajabi2026area}.

Most training-free fixes edit either the positional encoding~\citep{zhuo2024lumina,issachar2025dype,zhao2025ultraimage,rajabi2026sega} or the set of keys each query sees~\citep{zhang2023hidiffusion,ma2025resdit,xue2026hrdit,rajabi2026area}. AReA~\citep{rajabi2026area} is the most recent of the second kind. For the first 8 of 28 steps, full-resolution queries attend to keys and values subsampled onto a native-size grid, with query coordinates divided by the pooling factor; afterwards, queries attend inside windows that shrink geometrically to the native size, with a wavelet split test, a halo ring, random shifts, and a global key-value bridge to repair windowing artifacts. Every head of every layer receives the same receptive field at a given step, and the schedule is set by hand.

We question the shared receptive field. AReA's evidence for it, the narrowing of attention during denoising, is an average over heads and layers. We ask the pretrained model instead: at its training resolution, where full attention is trustworthy, how much of each head's output does a native-size neighborhood explain, and how much does a downsampled view of the whole image explain? A convex mix of the two explains each head well (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}). The weight differs strongly between heads, barely changes across prompts, and changes little with the emulated scale or the calibration resolution. We fit it once per model in closed form against the model's own full attention.

Output matching cannot set everything. Applied from the first step at $4096^2$, the calibrated mix duplicates subjects: one prompt for a Moai statue gives five statues (Figure~\ref{fig:failure}). At high noise only the lowest spatial frequencies carry signal, and these set layout and object count. A native window covers a quarter of the image width at $4096^2$, cannot resolve those frequencies, and invents a layout per window. Squared output error does not register this, because texture dominates it. \sca{} therefore runs the global branch alone while $\sigma>0.89$ (the first 8 of 28 steps) and the calibrated mix afterwards (Figure~\ref{fig:method}). The opening phase coincides with AReA's; the two methods differ in the remaining 20 steps.

The ablations separate the two parts. The opening phase is essential: without it, subjects repeat. After it, the calibration says that most heads lean local (mean weight 0.64 to 0.74), yet forcing every head to local-only attention lowers ImageReward by 0.36 and HPSv2.1 by 2.9 points. The minority weight each head puts on the global branch is what keeps global context in the last 20 steps. An earlier configuration with a low-band guard hid this, because the guard injects the global branch's low band into every head whatever its weight.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A protocol that emulates resolution extrapolation inside the training distribution and scores any receptive-field rule by its output error against full attention, from statistics collected in one pass.
  \item Evidence on FLUX.1-dev that receptive-field needs are head-specific, prompt-stable, and close to invariant under changes of scale and of calibration resolution.
  \item \sca{}, a training-free attention processor: a global-only opening phase, then a calibrated per-head mix of a native-grid global branch and an exact FlexAttention sliding window. One attention layer at $4096^2$ takes 18.7\,ms against 87.3\,ms for full attention.
  \item An evaluation on Aesthetic-4K at $4096^2$, $2048\times4096$, and $2048\times6144$ under one protocol shared with AReA's released code, with ablations of every component. \sca{} beats AReA at $4096^2$ and $2048\times6144$, matches it at $2048\times4096$, and is 2.7 to 3.0 times faster than AReA. AReA's paper reports ImageReward 1.32 at 4K under its own settings, so numbers across papers are not comparable.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Training-free high-resolution generation.}
For U-Nets, ScaleCrafter dilates convolutions~\citep{he2023scalecrafter}, HiDiffusion restructures the network and uses shifted windows~\citep{zhang2023hidiffusion}, FouriScale constrains the spectrum~\citep{huang2024fouriscale}, and \citet{jin2023training} rescale the attention temperature. DemoFusion~\citep{du2023demofusion}, FAM~\citep{yang2024fam}, and FreeScale~\citep{qiu2024freescale} fuse a global path with local patches; I-Max~\citep{du2024imax}, HiFlow~\citep{bu2025hiflow}, and ScaleDiff~\citep{koh2025scalediff} guide sampling with a native-resolution pass. Another line edits rotary embeddings~\citep{su2021roformer}: Lumina-Next~\citep{zhuo2024lumina}, DyPE~\citep{issachar2025dype}, UltraImage~\citep{zhao2025ultraimage}, SigMa~\citep{zhao2026sigma}, SEGA~\citep{rajabi2026sega}, and TIDE~\citep{liu2026tide}. The closest relatives of \sca{} change which keys a query sees. ResDiT fuses a global branch with scaled positions and patch-wise attention by Gaussian and Fourier weights~\citep{ma2025resdit}. HRDiT gives each head a static local scope chosen by an integer program under a cost budget~\citep{xue2026hrdit}. AReA schedules a shared receptive field from pooled-global to shrinking-local~\citep{rajabi2026area}. \sca{} differs from all three at once: its weights are per head and per noise level, its global branch is a zoom (pooling plus coordinate rescaling), and its weights are read off the model's own full attention instead of being set by hand or searched against a cost proxy.

\paragraph{Efficient and head-specific attention.}
ToDo attends from full-resolution queries to downsampled keys~\citep{smith2024todo}, the topology of our image-anchored branch without coordinate rescaling. DiTFastAttn selects window attention per layer and step~\citep{yuan2024ditfastattn}; for video, Sparse VideoGen classifies heads online~\citep{xi2025svg}, Sliding Tile Attention computes tiled windows efficiently~\citep{zhang2025sta}, and Sparse-vDiT searches per-head sparsity offline~\citep{chen2025sparsevdit}. These target speed at trained resolutions. Our local branch is blocked neighborhood attention~\citep{hassani2022nat} written in FlexAttention~\citep{dong2024flex}. Language models assign positions or spans per head~\citep{jin2024selfextend,zhang2024mspoe,fu2024moa,xiao2024duoattention,xiao2023streamingllm,peng2023yarn}, and trained vision transformers learn head-level scale splits~\citep{yang2021focal,wang2021pvt,pan2022hilo,ren2021shunted,liu2021swin}. Our measurements suggest that a full-attention diffusion transformer learns a comparable split implicitly, and \sca{} reads it out without training.

\section{Scale-Covariant Attention}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Setting}
A multimodal diffusion transformer such as FLUX.1 concatenates $T$ text tokens with $N=H\times W$ image tokens and applies joint attention in every block. FLUX.1 has 19 double-stream and 38 single-stream blocks with 24 heads each, and RoPE encodes each image token's grid position $p=(r,c)$. It is trained at about $1024^2$ pixels, a native grid of $n_0\times n_0$ tokens with $n_0=64$. For one head, full attention is
\begin{equation}
  \Ofull_i \;=\; \sum_{j} \frac{\exp(\ell_{ij})}{\sum_{j'}\exp(\ell_{ij'})}\, v_j,
  \qquad \ell_{ij} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{\sqrt d}\,\big(R_{p_i} q_i\big)^{\!\top} \big(R_{p_j} k_j\big),
\end{equation}
with $R_p$ the rotation for position $p$. The target grid is $H=s_H n_0$, $W=s_W n_0$ with integer scale factors, written $s$ when equal. The flow-matching latent at noise level $\sigma\in[0,1]$ is $x_\sigma=(1-\sigma)\,x_0+\sigma\,\epsilon$. A softmax over any partition of the keys into sets $A$ and $B$ splits exactly into
\begin{equation}
  \Ofull_i \;=\; m_i\, O^{A}_i + (1-m_i)\, O^{B}_i,
  \label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
where $O^{A}_i$, $O^{B}_i$ are the outputs restricted to $A$ and $B$ and $m_i$ is the softmax mass on $A$. With $A$ a neighborhood of the query, every head is a query-dependent mix of a near field and a far field. \sca{} replaces both restricted outputs with branches that stay in distribution, and the mass $m_i$ with a calibrated per-head weight.

\subsection{Two branches, two coordinate systems}

\paragraph{Pixel-anchored branch.} The grid is cut into query tiles of $t\times t$ tokens ($t=8$). Every query in a tile attends to all text keys and to the $n_0\times n_0$ window of image keys centered on the tile and clamped to the grid, with the original full-resolution positions. Each query sees $n_0^2+T$ keys, the native count, at relative offsets below $n_0$ per axis, the native range. This branch models content whose extent is fixed in tokens: edges, texture, fine structure. Its output is $\Oloc_i$.

\paragraph{Image-anchored branch.} Keys and values are subsampled at the center of every $s_H\times s_W$ block, giving $n_0^2$ pooled image keys. Pooled key $b$ takes the block index as its position and query $i$ takes $\lfloor p_i / s\rfloor$ per axis, so both share one native coordinate system. Text keys are not pooled. Each query again sees $n_0^2+T$ keys at native offsets. This branch models content whose extent is a fixed fraction of the image: layout, object count, identity. Its output is $\Oglob_i$. It is AReA's first phase with the pooling factor fixed at $s$~\citep{rajabi2026area}.

\paragraph{Per-head mixing with a noise gate.} For image queries of head $h$ at noise level $\sigma$,
\begin{equation}
  O_{i} \;=\; \pih\, \Oloc_i + \big(1-\pih\big)\, \Oglob_i,
  \qquad \pih = 0 \text{ for } \sigma>\sigma_g .
  \label{eq:mix}
\end{equation}
Text queries use the image-anchored branch alone. Relative to Equation~\ref{eq:split}, $\pih$ stands in for the near-field mass; it is constant across queries, which makes it cheap and lets a few prompts determine it.

\paragraph{Why the gate.} At noise level $\sigma$, the signal-to-noise ratio of a spatial frequency band is $((1-\sigma)/\sigma)^2 S(f)$ for image spectrum $S$. Natural spectra fall with frequency, so in the first steps only the lowest band carries signal, and that band fixes layout and object count. A window of $w$ tokens completes less than one period of any frequency below $1/w$ cycles per token, so it cannot tell one large object from a repeating pattern, and neighboring windows settle the question independently. Running the mix from the first step therefore duplicates subjects at the window's scale: with a 16-token window the duplicates shrink and multiply (Figure~\ref{fig:failure}). The image-anchored branch sees the whole image on the native grid and resolves this band. \sca{} sets $\pih=0$ and skips the pixel-anchored branch while $\sigma>\sigma_g=0.89$, which covers the first 8 of 28 steps at the maximum shift. This equals AReA's switch point $\sigma^\star$. Gating 13 steps ($\sigma_g=0.80$) produces a $4\times4$ block mosaic, because all queries in an $s\times s$ block share one coarse position in the image-anchored branch and receive nearly the same output at high noise. We have not measured the per-band signal-to-noise ratio of FLUX.1 latents; this argument is qualitative, and the gate value comes from generations, not from a spectral calculation.

\subsection{Calibration by output matching}
\label{sec:calib}
The weights must come from where the model is trustworthy, which the target resolution is not. We emulate extrapolation inside the training distribution: at native resolution, an emulated scale $s_c$ uses a local window of $(n_0/s_c)^2$ tokens, a pooling factor $s_c$ onto an $(n_0/s_c)^2$ grid, and query tiles of $t/s_c$. The ratio of image to window is then $s_c$, as at a real target of scale $s_c$, while full attention at native resolution stays in distribution and serves as the reference. For every block, head, and noise-level bin we accumulate, over prompts, steps, image queries, and channels,
\begin{equation}
  A=\textstyle\sum \|\Oloc-\Oglob\|^2,\quad
  B=\textstyle\sum \langle \Ofull-\Oglob,\, \Oloc-\Oglob\rangle,\quad
  E=\textstyle\sum \|\Ofull-\Oglob\|^2 .
\end{equation}
The squared error of the mix is $E-2\pi B+\pi^2 A$, so the optimal weight is $\pih=\operatorname{clip}(B/A,0,1)$. The same statistics score any other rule (per-layer weights, one shared weight per noise level, a hard switch at any threshold, local only, global only) without another forward pass. We use 14 uniform bins in $\sigma$ and interpolate linearly between bin centers; the table has $57\times24\times14$ entries. Calibration runs the unmodified model at $1024^2$, collects emulated scales 2 and 4 in one pass, and took about 1.5 GPU-hours on one GH200 in total for 32 prompts at $1024^2$ and 16 at $1280^2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:calib}).

\subsection{Inference and cost}
\label{sec:inference}
At a target with factors $(s_H,s_W)$, \sca{} uses the table of the emulated scale closest in log space to $\max(s_H,s_W)$; all our targets use the scale-4 table. Algorithm~\ref{alg:sca} in Appendix~\ref{app:impl} gives one layer. Weights, scheduler, VAE, and text encoders are untouched; the method is an attention-processor swap. Per head and block, full attention costs $(N+T)^2$ query-key pairs; \sca{} costs $N(n_0^2+T)$ for the pixel-anchored branch, skipped in 8 of 28 steps, and $(N+T)(n_0^2+T)$ for the image-anchored branch, both linear in $N$. At $4096^2$ this is 8.4 times fewer attention FLOPs than full attention (Appendix~\ref{app:impl}). The pixel-anchored branch is one FlexAttention call~\citep{dong2024flex} whose block mask is the window membership itself, so it is an exact sliding window rather than an approximation.

\section{What the heads want}
\label{sec:analysis}

We run the calibration on FLUX.1-dev with 32 prompts at $1024^2$, guidance 4.5, 28 steps, and the shift $\mu$ at its maximum (the setting of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), and repeat it with 16 prompts at $1280^2$. The error measure is the relative output error $\sum\|\Ofull-O\|^2/\sum\|\Ofull\|^2$ over image queries, pooled over blocks, heads, and steps.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/pi_heatmap.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Within every block, heads range from almost purely global to almost purely local.} Fitted $\pih$ for all 57 blocks and 24 heads of FLUX.1-dev at three noise levels, emulated scale 2, 32 prompts. Within each block, heads are sorted by their weight at $\sigma=0.54$, in the same order in all panels. The white line separates double-stream from single-stream blocks.}
  \label{fig:heatmap}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Heads differ.} At emulated scale 2 the standard deviation of $\pih$ over the 1368 heads is 0.24 to 0.26 at every noise level (Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}). Half of all (head, noise level) cells (50.4\%; 49.7\% at scale 4) lie in $[0.2,0.8]$, 11.7\% are above 0.95, and 2.9\% below 0.05. The mean weight is 0.64 to 0.74 depending on the noise level, so the typical head leans local but still draws on the global branch. The spread is mostly within blocks: a per-layer table recovers only part of the gain of the per-head table (Figure~\ref{fig:relerr} in Appendix~\ref{app:calib}). The median head changes its weight by 0.18 across noise levels, and a quarter of heads by more than 0.3.

\paragraph{The table is a property of the model, not of the prompts.} Tables fitted on two disjoint halves of the prompts correlate at 0.989 over all cells (0.986 at scale 4). Each half's table scored on the other half gives a relative error of 0.0576, against 0.0574 in sample. At $1280^2$, 16 prompts give a split-half correlation of 0.974.

\paragraph{The weights barely depend on scale or calibration resolution.} The table fitted at emulated scale 2 and applied at scale 4 gives a relative error of 0.205 against 0.200 for the scale-4 table: 2.4\% higher, and still below every shared rule fitted at scale 4 (Figure~\ref{fig:relerr}). The scale-4 table at scale 2 gives 0.0599 against 0.0574. Across calibration resolutions, the $1024^2$ table scored at $1280^2$ gives 0.0589 against 0.0585, and the reverse gives 0.0578 against 0.0574. The two resolutions emulate scale 2 with windows of 32 and 40 tokens, so the weights depend on the ratio of image to window rather than on the window's absolute size. This is what we mean by scale covariance: as the image grows, each branch follows its own law and the weight between them stays put.

\paragraph{Output error and what it misses.} Per-head mixing lowers the relative error from 0.092 to 0.057 at scale 2 and from 0.261 to 0.200 at scale 4, relative to the best shared hard switch; a per-layer table reaches 0.071 and 0.223, one shared weight per noise level 0.077 and 0.230. In output energy there is no global phase: the best hard switch never switches (Appendix~\ref{app:calib}). The gate of Section~\ref{sec:method} contradicts this on purpose, because output error is dominated by texture and does not weigh the low band that sets layout at high noise.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Model, sampler, baselines.} FLUX.1-dev~\citep{flux2024} in bf16, 28 flow-matching Euler steps, guidance 4.5, and the timestep shift $\mu$ at its maximum (1.15) at every resolution, with one fixed seed per prompt shared across methods and tiled VAE decoding for all. These are AReA's FLUX.1 defaults, applied to every method so that only attention differs. Baselines are full attention at the target resolution (Base) and AReA~\citep{rajabi2026area} from its released code under this protocol, with the FLUX.1-dev checkpoint in place of the FLUX.1-Krea-dev default of the release. Appendix~\ref{app:area} gives AReA's settings and why its paper's numbers are not comparable with ours. We did not run DyPE, SEGA, UltraImage, or HRDiT under this protocol, and we ran full attention only at $4096^2$.

\paragraph{Benchmark.} The 195 evaluation prompts of Aesthetic-4K with their 4K reference images~\citep{zhang2025diffusion4k}, at $4096\times4096$, $2048\times4096$, and $2048\times6144$ (height $\times$ width)\footnote{AReA's paper writes its sizes without stating the order (for example $6144\times2048$). Its released schedules are identical under transposition, so the orientation does not change AReA's computation; our wide sizes are landscape.}. Four of the 195 prompts (boats, Moai, woman and bear, underwater surfer) were used to choose the gate and window, so the main table reports the other 191. Ablations use a fixed subset of 46 of these.

\paragraph{\sca{} settings.} $n_0=64$, tile $t=8$, local window $64\times64$, block-center pooling, gate $\sigma_g=0.89$, and the scale-4 table from the 32-prompt calibration at $1024^2$. The calibration prompts are written for this purpose and share no prompt with Aesthetic-4K (the largest word-set Jaccard overlap with any evaluation prompt is 0.26). ``\sca{} + band guard'' additionally replaces the low band of each head's output by that of the image-anchored branch (Appendix~\ref{app:band}); it is the configuration of our first full run and not part of the final method.

\paragraph{Metrics.} ImageReward (IR)~\citep{xu2023imagereward}, LAION aesthetic score~\citep{schuhmann2022aesthetic}, CLIP score (ViT-L/14)~\citep{radford2021clip}, PickScore~\citep{kirstain2023pickapic}, HPSv2 and HPSv2.1~\citep{wu2023hpsv2}; FID~\citep{heusel2017fid} against the 4K references at $299^2$ and patch FID (FID$_\text{p}$) on four fixed $1024^2$ crops per image; MUSIQ~\citep{ke2021musiq} on the resized image and on a $1024^2$ crop (MUSIQ$_\text{c}$), CLIP-IQA~\citep{wang2022clipiqa}, and ARNIQA~\citep{agnolucci2024arniqa}. Intervals are 95\% percentile paired bootstraps over prompts with 1000 draws. Latency is wall-clock per image on one idle GH200 (96\,GB), batch 1, including text encoding and VAE decoding, after a warm-up generation.

\paragraph{The opening phase is essential.} Without the gate (v0, the calibrated mix from the first step), ImageReward falls by 1.33 $[1.06,1.60]$ against final \sca{} and patch FID rises by 34.7: subjects repeat across the image (Figure~\ref{fig:failure}). \paragraph{The global branch cannot work alone.} Global only loses on every metric (patch FID $+68.7$, ARNIQA $-45$): with keys $s$ tokens apart it cannot render detail. Mean pooling blurs the image-anchored keys (ImageReward $-1.52$), confirming AReA's choice of block-center subsampling.

\paragraph{Per-head weights beat shared and per-layer weights.} Without the guard, replacing the per-head table with one shared weight per noise level lowers ImageReward by 0.09 $[0.01,0.17]$, HPSv2.1 by 1.83, PickScore by 0.38, CLIP-IQA by 7.7, ARNIQA by 6.5, and MUSIQ$_\text{c}$ by 10.4, all significant. % =====================================================================================
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{Small scale factors.} At $2048\times4096$ \sca{} only matches AReA and is worse on aesthetic score, CLIP-IQA, and patch FID; the speed gain remains. \textbf{Residual duplication.} With the gate alone, small extra figures can appear late in sampling on some prompts, and the underwater surfer prompt keeps doubled limbs in every variant we tried; AReA's image for that prompt shows a large translucent ghost. The band guard (Appendix~\ref{app:band}) removes some of these at a cost. \textbf{The gate threshold} equals AReA's $\sigma^\star$, was compared only with 0.80 on four prompts, and its dependence on the shift and step count is untested. \textbf{Calibration objective.} Squared output error misses low-band errors, so the gate is set by hand. \textbf{Emulation.} Emulated scale 4 at $1024^2$ uses a 16-token window, the real $4096^2$ target a 64-token window. Transfer across emulated scales supports, but cannot prove, that the weights depend only on the ratio. \textbf{Scope.} All results are for FLUX.1-dev. We have not calibrated or evaluated a second multimodal diffusion transformer, so the claim that receptive-field needs are head-specific and scale-stable is shown for one model.

% =====================================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

At its training resolution, FLUX.1-dev shows how each attention head splits between a neighborhood fixed in tokens and a view of the whole image; the split differs between heads, holds across prompts, and changes little with scale, so it can be measured once and reused at 4K. Output matching sets the weights but not the opening phase, in which every head must see the whole image. With both, \sca{} beats AReA's released code at $4096^2$ (ImageReward 1.23 against 0.92, FID 138.4 against 146.0) while running 3.0 times faster, ties it at $2048\times4096$, and beats it at $2048\times6144$ with the largest FID gain ($-19.8$).

\section{Calibration details}
\label{app:calib}

\paragraph{Statistics.} For every emulated scale, block, head, and noise-level bin, the pass stores $A$, $B$, $E$, the error of the local branch alone, $\sum\|\Ofull\|^2$, and the query count, in float64, summed over image queries and channels. Statistics are written per prompt and merged by summation, so prompts can be added later and split-half fits need no extra pass. Text-query outputs are not in the objective, because \sca{} sends text queries through the image-anchored branch only.

\paragraph{Geometry at emulated scale.} At $1024^2$ the grid is $64\times64$. Emulated scale 2 uses a $32\times32$ window, $2\times2$ pooling onto a $32\times32$ grid, and $4\times4$ query tiles; emulated scale 4 uses a $16\times16$ window, $4\times4$ pooling onto a $16\times16$ grid, and $2\times2$ tiles. At $1280^2$ the grid is $80\times80$, and emulated scale 2 uses a $40\times40$ window and $2\times2$ pooling onto a $40\times40$ grid.

\paragraph{Noise-level bins.} Bins have width $1/14$. With 28 steps and $\mu=1.15$, $\sigma$ runs from 1.0 to 0.105, so the lowest bin is empty and copies its neighbor; 13 bins are populated. Bin centers are the mean $\sigma$ of their steps. Under this schedule 8 steps have $\sigma>0.89$ (the ninth has $\sigma=0.888$) and 13 have $\sigma>0.80$.

\paragraph{No global phase in output energy.} The best single shared weight falls from 0.88 at $\sigma=0.10$ to 0.72 at $\sigma=0.97$, and the mean per-head weight from 0.74 to 0.64 (Figure~\ref{fig:pisigma}). The global share rises with noise, but at no noise level does the average head prefer the global branch. Among hard switches from global to local, the best threshold is $\sigma^\star=1$, which equals local only. A switch at $\sigma^\star=0.89$ between our two branches raises the error from 0.092 to 0.152 at scale 2 and from 0.261 to 0.376 at scale 4. In the noisiest bin, the global branch alone has relative error 0.27 at scale 2, the local branch alone 0.14, and the per-head mix 0.09. The generation results of Section~\ref{sec:ablations} show the opposite preference for the opening steps, which is the gap between squared output error and layout.

\paragraph{Attention mass.} For 256 random image queries per block and step, the pass also records the softmax mass on text keys and on image keys within Chebyshev distance 2, 4, 8, and 16. At $1024^2$, averaged over heads and queries, text receives 15 to 19\% of the mass; image keys within distance 16 receive 52\% at $\sigma=0.10$ and 40\% at $\sigma=0.97$, and keys outside that $33\times33$ neighborhood still receive 29\% and 41\%. A purely local receptive field cannot account for any step even at native resolution.

\section{Implementation details and cost}
\label{app:impl}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
  \caption{One \sca{} attention layer at noise level $\sigma$ (per head $h$).}
  \label{alg:sca}
  \small
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require pre-RoPE $Q,K,V$ for $T$ text and $N=H\times W$ image tokens; factors $(s_H,s_W)$; table $\pi_h(\sigma)$; gate $\sigma_g=0.89$
    \State $\tilde K,\tilde V \gets$ image keys and values at the center of every $s_H\times s_W$ block, plus all text keys
    \State $\Oglob \gets \mathrm{Attn}\big(Q\text{ at }\lfloor p/s\rfloor,\;\tilde K\text{ at block index},\;\tilde V\big)$ \Comment{all queries, text included}
    \If{$\sigma>\sigma_g$} \Return $\Oglob$ \Comment{opening phase: pixel-anchored branch skipped}
    \EndIf
    \State $\Oloc \gets$ FlexAttention of each $t\times t$ image-query tile over text keys and its $n_0\times n_0$ window, original RoPE
    \State \Return $[\,\Oglob_{\text{text}},\;\pih\,\Oloc + (1-\pih)\,\Oglob_{\text{img}}\,]$ \Comment{mix in fp32}
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{FlexAttention window.} Image tokens are reordered tile-major; query blocks cover $8\times16$ tokens and key blocks $8\times8$, shifted so that unclamped windows start on a block edge, and the mask function is the window membership itself. The kernel matches a reference that gathers each tile's key window explicitly to within one bf16 unit in the last place, and both are equally far from an fp32 reference. RoPE, pooling, both branches, and the mix compile into one graph per layer.

\paragraph{Paired intervals for Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.} The intervals of every row in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} are paired bootstraps over the 46 prompts with 2000 draws and seed 0, computed by \texttt{scripts/paired\_bootstrap.py} from the per-image scores against final \sca{}; the outputs are \texttt{results/ablnb\_<method>\_deltas.csv}. Patch FID is a set-level metric (\texttt{results/ablnb\_base\_summary.csv}) and has no paired interval.

\paragraph{Position ids in bf16.} The FLUX.1 pipeline builds image position ids in bf16, which cannot represent every integer above 256, so positions beyond 256 tokens per axis are rounded. This affects every method, including full attention and AReA, at the $2048\times6144$ setting (384 tokens per row). \sca{} takes the grid size from the sampler instead of inferring it from the rounded ids, so that its window and pooling geometry match the true grid.

\section{The low-band duplication guard}
\label{app:band}

\paragraph{Construction.} Let $\LP$ average-pool a head's image output over $p\times p$ tokens and upsample it bilinearly, and $\HP=\mathrm{I}-\LP$. The guard replaces the low band of the mix $M$ by that of the image-anchored branch:
\begin{equation}
  O \;=\; \LP\big(\Oglob\big) + \HP\big(M\big) \;=\; \Oglob + \pih\,\HP\big(\Oloc-\Oglob\big).
\end{equation}
The pixel-anchored branch then contributes only its difference from the image-anchored branch above the cutoff. The cutoff should lie below $1/(2s)$, where the image-anchored branch aliases, and above $1/w$, below which the window cannot resolve; with $w=64$ and $s=4$ this leaves periods of 8 to 64 tokens. We used $p=16$; $p=8$ and $p=32$ were worse on four development prompts, and a noise-dependent cutoff behaved like $p=16$. FreeScale~\citep{qiu2024freescale} also fuses two self-attention outputs by frequency band, in the opposite direction: it keeps the Gaussian-blurred low band of attention over overlapping crops and takes the high band from full self-attention at the target size, to suppress small repeated objects. The guard instead takes the low band from the image-anchored branch, which stays at native token count and offsets, and keeps the per-head weight on the high band.

\paragraph{What it fixes and what it costs.} On the development prompts, the band alone (no gate) fixes the boats but leaves extra statues and faces on the Moai and woman-and-bear prompts; the gate alone gets the main subject right but small extra figures appear late on some prompts; gate and band together give one subject on the boats, Moai, and woman-and-bear prompts. On 46 prompts, however, removing the guard improves HPSv2 ($+0.54$), HPSv2.1 ($+1.17$), PickScore ($+0.25$), CLIP-IQA ($+1.7$), and MUSIQ ($+2.9$), all significant, leaves ImageReward, aesthetic score, ARNIQA, MUSIQ$_\text{c}$, and patch FID within noise, and saves 17\,s per image on an idle GPU. The guard also hides what the per-head mix contributes. With it (Table~\ref{tab:ablation_band}), local only matched the per-head mix within 2\% on preference metrics and was slightly higher on HPSv2.1 ($+0.55$), because the guard replaces the low band of every head's output with that of the image-anchored branch whatever the head's weight, so it injects global low frequencies even at $\pi=1$; without the guard local only loses on nearly every metric (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}). Likewise, with the guard one shared weight and per-layer weights matched the per-head table on preference metrics and lost only on no-reference quality, and hard per-head routing (10 to 17\% of heads global at threshold 0.5, about 6\% at 0.3) stayed within 2\% of the mix on preference metrics. The guard is therefore an option for prompts where late duplication is visible, not part of the default method.

\section{Reproducing AReA}
\label{app:area}

We run AReA's released code with its FLUX.1 defaults (block-center pooling, first phase for 8 of 28 steps, halo width 8, bridge ratio 0.5), the FLUX.1-dev checkpoint, guidance 4.5, 28 steps, $\mu$ at its maximum, and the seeds shared with the other methods. AReA's paper reports ImageReward 1.32 at 4K under its own settings; our shared protocol gives 0.92, so cross-paper numbers are not comparable, while all numbers in this paper come from one protocol. AReA's paper reports 151\,s for AReA and 314\,s for full attention at $4096^2$ on an H100; we measure 212.6\,s and 184.6\,s on a single GH200 (medians of three images). We use commit \texttt{9e38ec2} of the released repository (github.com/KooroshRH/AReA), which includes the cost cap on halo and bridge tokens and the jagged halo edge; neither appears in the paper text, and the paper does not say whether its Table~1 used them. Recomputed from the code with 512 text tokens, the schedules are as follows. At $4096\times4096$ and $2048\times4096$, the first-phase pooling factors are 4, 4, 4, 4, 2, 2, 2, 2; at $2048\times6144$ they are 8, 8, 8, 4, 4, 4, 2, 2. The second-phase windows shrink over 20 steps from 180, 151, and 192 tokens respectively to 64. At $2048\times6144$ no window is cheaper than full attention once the text tokens are counted, so the first window falls back to 192 tokens and the cap drops the bridge and halo on the first three second-phase steps. Other differences from the release defaults: the FLUX.1-dev checkpoint instead of FLUX.1-Krea-dev, and one seed per prompt shared across methods instead of one seed for all prompts.
